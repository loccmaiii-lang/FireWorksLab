Intended direction: A dense, dark, utilitarian workstation for people who live in it all day. Everything is meant to be one click away. Tiled, tabbed panes surround a large central 3D viewport, with a parameter inspector and a node graph stacked on the right and a timeline across the bottom. The charcoal chrome is supposed to step back so the content leads. The viewport gets a pale blue-grey "sky" gradient so geometry reads against it. Color is supposed to help you recognise tools at a glance on two rows of shelf toolbars, and node types in the graph. Density is the point, and it suits the audience. The problem is not how much is on screen. It is that so many unrelated visual voices compete for attention at once.
Contrast with Pentagram / Apple / Teenage Engineering: Pentagram would keep the same density but impose a system on it: one typeface, three sizes, a strict baseline grid, and section headers with real typographic weight. The shelves would become a disciplined set of text labels instead of a wall of mixed clip-art. Apple (Final Cut Pro, Logic, Reality Composer Pro) would use one monochrome symbol set at a single weight. It would merge the toolbars into one unified toolbar and hide rarely used chrome until it is needed. The inspector would get clear grouped sections with consistent 8-pt spacing, and the viewport and chrome would sit in the same tonal family. Teenage Engineering would go further still: monochrome everything, one or two accent colors that only signal state, large confident numerals in the parameter fields, and a timeline built to feel like a precise instrument rather than two stacked strips of tiny ticks. All three would treat color strictly as information. Here color is mostly decoration on icons, and none of them would allow three different chrome zones above the viewport.
Biggest gaps:
1. The icon system is incoherent. There are well over 150 icons in at least four styles: glossy 3D primitives, flat glyphs, outline glyphs and colored clip-art. Hues, optical sizes and stroke weights all vary. The vertical toolbars down both sides of the viewport read as rainbow confetti, and nothing makes the important tools stand out from the trivial ones.
2. Type is tiny and has no real hierarchy. Shelf labels, shelf tabs, pane tabs, timeline numbers and parameter labels are all around 8–10 px, mid-grey on charcoal, with low contrast. The only large type on the whole screen is a decorative "Objects" watermark and the node names. Section headers ("Source", "Pyro Solver", "Shape", "Advanced") look almost the same as field labels and are set apart only by a faint band. Numeric fields show raw precision such as "0.0806667".
3. Chrome overload and redundancy. About 130 px of stacked chrome sits above the viewport: the menu bar, two rows of shelf tabs, a shelf of icons, pane tabs and a path bar. The "obj" path bar appears three times. Every pane tab has its own close "x". The timeline is two separate strips. Wherever the eye lands, it lands on chrome before content.
4. The tonal split is jarring. A large, light blue-grey gradient viewport sits inside near-black chrome, so the eye keeps jumping between two luminance worlds. The yellow selection outline on pale blue has weak contrast. Grid numerals in pink and blue float without purpose, and the permanent navigation help sentence along the bottom of the viewport is low-contrast noise.
5. The parameter panel has no rhythm. Hairline slider tracks stretch roughly 400 px for 0–1 values, with thumbs lost in empty space. The tab row (Simulation / Source / Visualisation / Transform) is visually weak, and its "Source" duplicates the "Source" section header just below. The last row ("Max Substeps") is cut off at the pane edge.
6. The network editor is mostly empty. Hundreds of pixels of dark field hold three small nodes with arbitrary body colors (olive, blue, acid yellow-green), and the selected node's glowing outline is the loudest element in that pane for no reason.
7. Micro-spacing is uneven. Icon gaps in the side toolbars, the group dividers, the shelf label baselines and the tab padding all drift. Small "Persp" / "No cam" chips and stray ▾ arrows are scattered around the panes with no shared grammar.
AI tells: none. This is a hand-accreted, long-lived production UI, and its faults are the opposite of AI genericness. There are no purple gradients, no card-within-card, no hero layout. The closest look-alikes are functional: the viewport gradient is a deliberate horizon cue, the rounded node bodies are graph nodes rather than pill tags, and the "Persp" / "No cam" chips are real dropdowns.
Top 3 to delete / simplify:
1. Delete the full-color tool icons from the shelves and side toolbars and replace them with one monochrome glyph set at one weight. Keep color only for state (active, selected, error, cooking). Cut the two rows of shelf tabs to one row of visible shelves, with the rest moved behind a single tab menu or a command palette.
2. Remove the redundancy: the duplicate "obj" path bars (keep one per pane only where it is needed), the close "x" on every tab (show it on hover only), the second timeline strip (merge the playback controls and range into one bar), and the permanent mouse-help sentence in the viewport.
3. Simplify the inspector. Give the sliders a short fixed width beside the field instead of a full-pane hairline. Give section headers real weight and spacing, drop the redundant section label where it repeats the tab name, and format the numbers to a sensible precision.
Score: 4/10 - The information density is well tested and suits specialists, but mismatched icon styles, tiny low-contrast type, redundant chrome and the tonal clash between viewport and chrome put it well below average agency polish.
